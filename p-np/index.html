P vs NP

P vs NP is a question about puzzles whose answers are quick to check. It asks: if an answer is quick to check, is it also quick to find?

Nobody knows. The question has been open since 1971, and there is a million-dollar prize for settling it.

Think of a Sudoku. Checking a finished grid takes a minute or two: look along each row, each column and each box. Solving one can take an hour.

Passwords have the same shape. Checking a guess takes an instant. Finding the correct one without knowing it means trying one possibility after another. Online banking, private messages and software updates all rely on that gap between checking and finding.

There might be a clever method, not yet discovered, that finds answers almost as fast as checking them.

1. One puzzle, two jobs

You get some tiles with numbers on them, and a target. An answer is a set of tiles that adds up to exactly the target.

There are two different jobs you can do with a puzzle like this. Solving means working out which tiles to pick. Checking means someone hands you some tiles and says “these work”, and you add them up to see.

Try solving first. Tap a tile to pick it, and tap it again to put it back. The page keeps the total for you, so there is no arithmetic to do: your only job is choosing. On this page, anything with a raised edge can be tapped.

2. A bigger puzzle is much slower to solve and barely slower to check

From here on, a “bigger” puzzle means one with more tiles to choose from. The target can be anything.

Checking stays easy as the puzzle grows. With 90 tiles, the most you would ever do is add up 90 numbers.

Solving by trial and error is different. Call each possible choice of tiles a combination. Every tile is either in the combination or out of it. With two tiles, A and B, there are four possible combinations.

Add a third tile, C. Each of those four combinations can stay as it is, or have C added. That makes eight.

3. Some versions of the puzzle can be solved without trial and error

Trying every combination is the least clever way to solve the puzzle. Sometimes there is a much better way.

The tiles are now 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on: each one is twice the one before. Call them the 1-2-4-8 tiles. The target is still 200.

The method is called biggest first. Look for the biggest tile that still fits in what is left to make, and take it. Repeat until nothing is left to make.

Solve it with “biggest first”: tap the biggest tile that fits

Notice that you went along the row once and never had to put a tile back. It works because each tile is bigger than all the smaller tiles put together: 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 + 64 is only 127, which is less than 128. So the biggest tile that fits has to go in, because the smaller ones could never make up the difference.

4. The same method fails on other tiles

Now go back to the tiles from the first puzzle, lined up from biggest to smallest. Same target, same method: tap the biggest tile that fits, and keep going.

Try “biggest first” again: make 200 from the original tiles

Notice what went wrong. With these tiles, taking one that fits can be a mistake, and nothing tells you so until later. So you put it back and try something else, again and again. That putting back is what trial and error is, and it is why the work doubles with every extra tile.

A shortcut is a method like “biggest first” on the 1-2-4-8 tiles: it solves the puzzle without all the putting back, so each extra tile adds only a little work. The 1-2-4-8 tiles have a shortcut. For tiles with any numbers, nobody has found one. Nobody has proven that none exists, either.

5. Two names: quick-check and quick-solve

From here on, quick means that each extra tile adds only a little work. Checking is quick: one more tile means one more number to add up. Trying every combination is not: one more tile means twice as many combinations.

A puzzle is quick-check if, when someone hands you an answer, checking it is quick. The name says nothing about how hard the answer was to find. The official name for these puzzles is NP.

A puzzle is quick-solve if finding an answer is quick too, because it has a shortcut. The official name for these is P. Anything quick-solve is quick-check too, so P sits inside NP.

Some puzzles are not quick-check: even checking an answer is slow. As far as anyone knows, chess is one.

The puzzle is to find the best move. Someone hands you an answer: “this move is the best one”. With the tiles, you could check an answer by adding it up. A chess move gives you nothing like that to check. The obvious way to be sure is to work through every reply your opponent could make, then every reply to each of those, and so on. Nobody has found a quick way.

P and NP are short for longer names. If you want to know what the letters stand for, that is explained at the end of the page.

6. P vs NP: is every quick-check puzzle also quick-solve?

Some puzzles are quick-check, and nobody knows whether they are quick-solve: tiles with any numbers, a giant Sudoku, a school timetable. P vs NP is a question about exactly these puzzles. Do they have shortcuts that nobody has found yet, or do they have none?

One possibility is that every one of them has a shortcut waiting to be found. Then every quick-check puzzle is quick-solve, the two names describe exactly the same puzzles, and P = NP.

The other possibility is that some of them have no shortcut at all. They are quick-check and slow-solve, whatever method you use. Then P ≠ NP, which reads “P is not equal to NP”.

Which picture is correct? Nobody knows.

Nobody knows which is true. Most researchers bet on the second: in a poll of computer scientists published in 2019, 88% of those who answered expected that P ≠ NP. But proving it means ruling out every clever method that anyone could ever invent, and nobody has managed that for even one quick-check puzzle.

7. Back to the password

The password from the top of the page is a quick-check puzzle. As far as anyone knows, finding it is slow, in the same way that solving tiles with any numbers is slow.

Online banking, private messages and software updates are all protected by puzzles of this kind. That protection rests on a belief that has never been proven: that these puzzles have no shortcut.

If P = NP, the belief is wrong. Every quick-check puzzle has a shortcut, and finding a password is one of them. The same goes for many scheduling and planning problems that have no known shortcut today.

If P ≠ NP, some quick-check puzzles have no shortcut at all. That would not prove that the puzzles behind passwords are among them, but it is the least that today's protection needs to be true.

One puzzle can also fall on its own. Today's protection rests on a handful of particular puzzles. If someone found a shortcut for one of those, that protection would break, and P vs NP would still be open.

Explain it back

Say each answer out loud before you open it.

A friend asks what P vs NP is. What do you say?

Some puzzles are quick-check: hand me an answer and I can confirm it with a little work. Some are also quick-solve, because they have a shortcut. P vs NP asks whether every quick-check puzzle is also quick-solve. Nobody knows, and most researchers think the answer is no.

Why does trying every combination stop working as the puzzle grows?

Every extra tile doubles the number of possible combinations. By 90 tiles, a computer trying a billion combinations a second would need about three times the age of the universe.

Why did “biggest first” work on the 1-2-4-8 tiles and fail on the others?

With the 1-2-4-8 tiles, the biggest tile that fits has to go in, because the smaller ones can't make up the difference. So every choice is safe and you never put a tile back. With other tiles, taking one that fits can be a mistake you only discover later.

Would much faster computers settle P vs NP?

No. Speed doesn't change which puzzles have a shortcut. When the work doubles with every tile, a computer a million times faster only gets you about 20 tiles further.

What the letters P and NP stand for

P stands for “polynomial time”. That is the precise name for what this page calls quick.

“Time” means the number of steps a method takes. “Polynomial” is about how that number grows as the puzzle gets bigger. Work is polynomial if it is never more than the size of the puzzle multiplied by itself a fixed number of times. With 20 tiles, that could be 20 steps, or 20 × 20 = 400 steps, or 20 × 20 × 20 = 8,000 steps.

What polynomial rules out is the growth from step 2, where the work doubles with every extra tile. Here is how far apart the two kinds of growth end up.

P is the group of puzzles that can be solved in polynomial time. Those are the quick-solve puzzles.

NP stands for “nondeterministic polynomial time”. It is the precise name for quick-check: the group of puzzles where an answer can be checked in polynomial time. It does not stand for “not P”, although that is a common way to misread it.

The word “nondeterministic” comes from how the group was first defined. An ordinary computer is deterministic: it follows its instructions one step at a time and never guesses. Researchers imagined a nondeterministic computer, one that is allowed to guess and always guesses correctly. Call it the lucky computer.

Give the lucky computer a tile puzzle. For each tile it guesses “take it” or “leave it”, and every guess is correct. Then it adds up the tiles it took, to confirm that they hit the target. It did no searching, so its only real work was the adding up. That is the same work as checking an answer.

So a puzzle that the lucky computer can solve in polynomial time is a puzzle whose answers can be checked in polynomial time, and the other way round.

Nobody can build a lucky computer. It was a way of defining the group, and it lives on only in the name.

Puzzles that are not quick-check have no single name. People say they are “outside NP”. Researchers do have names for several groups of harder puzzles, but none of those names simply means “not quick-check”.

What this page left out

  • A shortcut can still be slow. “Quick” on this page is about how the work grows as tiles are added, not about how long it takes. A method can be quick in that sense and still be far too slow to use. So a proof that P = NP would not automatically break anything. That would depend on how fast the shortcut turned out to be.
  • Chess on a bigger board. “Quick” is about how the work grows as a puzzle gets bigger, and ordinary chess never gets bigger. The claims about chess on this page are about chess on a board of any size.
  • Small numbers are easier. For tiles with any numbers, there is a known quick method when the target is small: it keeps a list of every total you can reach. The hard cases have long numbers on the tiles.
  • The hardest of these puzzles are tied together. Thousands of quick-check puzzles, tiles with any numbers among them, share a special property: every quick-check puzzle can be quickly turned into any one of them. So if one of them has a shortcut, every quick-check puzzle has one. They are called NP-complete.
  • How passwords are protected in practice. The password example is simplified. A website checks your guesses for you, and can lock you out after a few. The puzzle on this page is the one faced by someone who has stolen the site's scrambled copy of the password and can test guesses on their own machine.
  • Quantum computers. A large enough quantum computer could quickly solve the particular puzzles that protect much of today's banking and messaging, whatever the answer to P vs NP. By 2025, web browsers had started switching to different puzzles, which are believed to be safe from quantum computers.
