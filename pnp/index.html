P vs NP, from scratch

One small puzzle, eight steps, and the biggest open question in computer science. About 15 minutes.

Think of a long password. Checking whether a guess is right takes an instant. Finding the right one without knowing it means trying every possibility, and for a long password that would take longer than the universe has existed. Online banking, private messages and software updates all rely on that gap: quick to check, slow to find.

Nobody has ever proved that the gap is real. There might be a clever method, not yet discovered, that finds the answer almost as fast as checking it. If there is, most of the security on the internet stops working. So would much of what makes scheduling, design and even mathematics hard, because the same method would crack those too.

Whether such a method exists is the biggest open question in computer science. It is called P vs NP. This page builds it up from one small puzzle, and you need no math beyond adding up.

1. One puzzle, two jobs

Here is the puzzle we'll use all the way through. You get some tiles with numbers on them, and a target. An answer is a set of tiles that adds up to exactly the target.

There are two different jobs you can do with a puzzle like this. Solving means working out which tiles to pick. Checking means someone hands you some tiles and says “these work”, and you add them up to see.

Try solving first. Tap a tile to pick it, and tap it again to put it back. On this page, anything with a raised edge can be tapped.

Solve it: pick tiles that add up to exactly 200

Now try checking. This puzzle is bigger: 12 tiles, and a target of 1,400. Two people each say they have an answer. Either of them could be wrong. Add up their tiles to find out.

Check it: do these tiles add up to exactly 1,400?

Total so far: 0Target: 1,400

Notice the difference between the two jobs. Solving took trial and error. Checking took five taps, and you didn't have to trust anyone.

2. A bigger puzzle is much slower to solve and barely slower to check

From here on, a “bigger” puzzle means one with more tiles to choose from. The target can be anything.

Checking stays easy as the puzzle grows. With 90 tiles, the most you would ever do is add up 90 numbers.

Solving by trial and error is different. Call each possible choice of tiles a pick. Every tile is either in your pick or out of it. With two tiles, A and B, there are four possible picks:

no tilesABA B

3. Some versions of the puzzle can be solved without trial and error

Trying every pick is the least clever way to solve the puzzle. Sometimes there is a much better way. Here is a version of the same puzzle where there is.

The tiles are now 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on: each one is double the one before. The target is still 200.

The method is called biggest first. Look for the biggest tile that still fits in what is left to make, and take it. Repeat until nothing is left to make.

Solve it with “biggest first”: tap the biggest tile that fits

Notice that you went along the row once and never had to put a tile back. It works because each tile is bigger than all the smaller tiles put together: 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 + 64 is only 127, which is less than 128. So the biggest tile that fits has to go in, because the smaller ones could never make up the difference.

4. The same method fails on other tiles

Now go back to the tiles from the first puzzle, lined up from biggest to smallest. Same target, same method: tap the biggest tile that fits, and keep going.

Try “biggest first” again: make 200 from the original tiles

Notice what went wrong. With these tiles, taking one that fits can be a mistake, and nothing tells you so until later. So you put it back and try something else, again and again. That putting back is what trial and error is, and it is why the work doubles with every extra tile.

A shortcut is a method like “biggest first” on the doubling tiles: it solves the puzzle without all the putting back, so each extra tile adds only a little work. The doubling tiles have a shortcut. For tiles that can be any numbers, nobody has found one. Nobody has proved that none exists, either.

5. Two names: quick-check and quick-solve

Now two names, built the same way.

A puzzle is quick-check if, when someone hands you an answer, you can check it with a little work, like adding up tiles. The name says nothing about how hard the answer was to find.

A puzzle is quick-solve if you can also find an answer with a little work, because it has a shortcut. Anything quick-solve is quick-check too.

Not every puzzle is even quick-check. Suppose someone tells you “this is the best possible move in this chess position”. There is nothing to add up. To be sure, you would have to play out every reply.

Now try it on four puzzles you haven't seen here. For each one, ask yourself two things. If someone handed me an answer, could I check it quickly? And is there a known way to solve it without trial and error?

Which kind of puzzle is each of these?

Computer scientists have their own names for these. They call the quick-solve puzzles P and the quick-check puzzles NP. Since anything quick-solve is quick-check too, P sits inside NP.

6. P vs NP: is every quick-check puzzle also quick-solve?

Some puzzles are quick-check, and nobody knows whether they are quick-solve: tiles with any numbers, a giant Sudoku, a school timetable. P vs NP is a question about exactly these puzzles. Do they have shortcuts that nobody has found yet, or do they have none?

One possibility is that every one of them has a shortcut waiting to be found. Then every quick-check puzzle is quick-solve, the two names describe exactly the same puzzles, and P = NP.

The other possibility is that some of them have no shortcut at all. They are quick-check and slow-solve, whatever method you use. Then P ≠ NP.

Nobody knows which is true. Most researchers bet on the second. But proving it means ruling out every clever method that anyone could ever invent, and nobody has managed that for even one puzzle.

7. Thousands of puzzles turn out to be one puzzle

Here is a puzzle that looks different from the tile puzzle. Ana and Ben have eight tiles to share. They want it fair: Ana's tiles must add up to the same as Ben's.

The eight tiles add up to 400 altogether, so each of them needs exactly 200.

Share the tiles fairly between Ana and Ben

Tap the tiles that go to Ana. Ben gets the rest.

Ana has 0
Ben has 400

The split is fair when both totals are the same.

Notice what you just did. To share the tiles fairly, you had to find tiles that add up to exactly 200. That is the tile puzzle from step 1, with a target of 200. The fair-share puzzle is the tile puzzle in different clothes.

So any method that solves the tile puzzle also solves fair sharing: work out half the total, then use that as the target. A shortcut for the tile puzzle would be a shortcut for fair sharing, for free.

In the 1970s researchers found that this kind of rewriting goes much further than you would expect. With enough ingenuity, a giant Sudoku can be rewritten as a tile puzzle, a school timetable as a Sudoku, and so on, across thousands of puzzles that look nothing alike. The rewriting itself is quick. So a shortcut for any one of them would be a shortcut for all of them.

These are the hardest quick-check puzzles: every quick-check puzzle there is can be rewritten as one of them. Their technical name is NP-complete.

That shrinks the whole question down to one puzzle. P = NP exactly when the any-number tile puzzle has a shortcut.

8. Back to the password

Finding a long password is quick-check and, as far as anyone knows, slow-solve. That is the same lopsided shape as the tile puzzle, and it is what the security of the internet is built on.

If the hardest quick-check puzzles turned out to have a shortcut, that protection would be gone. Part 2 is about how encryption is built from puzzles like these, and what would break.

Explain it back

Say each answer out loud before you open it.

A friend asks what P vs NP is. What do you say?

Some puzzles are quick-check: hand me an answer and I can confirm it with a little work. Some are also quick-solve, because they have a shortcut. P vs NP asks whether every quick-check puzzle is also quick-solve. Nobody knows, and most researchers think the answer is no.

Why does trying every pick stop working as the puzzle grows?

Every extra tile doubles the number of possible picks. By 90 tiles, a computer trying a billion picks a second would need about three times the age of the universe.

Why did “biggest first” work on the doubling tiles and fail on the others?

With doubling tiles, the biggest tile that fits has to go in, because the smaller ones can't make up the difference. So every choice is safe and you never put a tile back. With other tiles, taking one that fits can be a mistake you only discover later.

Why would a shortcut for one puzzle settle the whole question?

Because the hardest quick-check puzzles can all be rewritten as one another, and every quick-check puzzle can be rewritten as one of them. A shortcut for any one of them is a shortcut for everything.
